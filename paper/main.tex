\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are being used to screen applications and to explain their decisions. A model can treat applicants differently because of who they appear to be while writing explanations that never mention identity, a failure that an auditor reading explanations alone would miss. We study this for implicit Indian identity signals. We build 150 synthetic applications (software-engineering shortlisting, personal loans, merit-cum-means scholarships) and create paired variants that differ only in a caste-coded surname, a religion-coded name, a region-coded name and hometown, or a gender-coded first name, with same-group name swaps as a noise floor, in English and Hindi (6{,}600 prompts per model). We audit eight open-weight models, including a Qwen3 family at four sizes and an Indic model. For each model we measure (i)~shifts in the probability of a \emph{Yes} decision, (ii)~whether a reason-first explanation names the identity attribute when the decision changes, and (iii)~whether the model, asked directly, correctly predicts whether its own decision would change. A manipulation check tests whether models decode the intended attribute from each name. \TODO{RQ1/RQ2 headline: size of decision shifts relative to the control noise floor, and English vs Hindi}. \TODO{RQ3 headline: disclosure rate on identity-driven flips vs control flips; self-prediction balanced accuracy/AUROC vs the margin baseline}. \TODO{RQ4 headline: trend across Qwen3 4B--32B}. We release code and, under gated access, the benchmark.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

LLMs are increasingly asked to make or support allocation decisions such as who is shortlisted, who gets a loan and who receives a scholarship, and they are routinely asked to justify these decisions. Two questions matter for anyone relying on such a system. First, does the decision depend on who the applicant appears to be? Second, if it does, will the model's explanation, or the model itself when asked, reveal this? If the answer to the first is yes and to the second is no, then reading explanations gives false reassurance.

Correspondence studies have long measured differential treatment by sending otherwise identical applications that differ only in a name~\cite{bertrand2004emily}, including caste- and religion-coded names in India~\cite{banerjee2009labor,siddique2011evidence}. The same paired design has been applied to LLMs~\cite{tamkin2023evaluating,an2024large,armstrong2024silicon,salinas2024whats}, but mostly with US race and gender signals. Indian identity is signalled differently: caste and religion are typically inferred from surnames, region from names and hometowns, and the signals are entangled with language and class~\cite{sambasivan2021reimagining,bhatt2022recontextualizing}. Earlier LLM audits that use Indian names disagree: a GPT-4o hiring study with Indian-name swaps found no significant caste or region effect in hiring evaluations~\cite{rao2025invisible}, whereas a ChatGPT study reports dominant-caste preference in shortlisting~\cite{zaveri2025caste}.

Separately, work on explanation faithfulness shows that chain-of-thought and post-hoc explanations can omit the factor that actually drove an answer~\cite{turpin2023language,lanham2023measuring,chen2025reasoning,matton2025walk}. Most relevant, Arcuschin et al.~\cite{arcuschin2026biases} test whether models verbalise concepts that significantly change hiring, loan and admissions decisions. Their identity concepts are Western or stated explicitly. We ask the same question when the identity signal is \emph{implicit}: an Indian surname or name, which a model must first decode before it can either act on it or report it.

\textbf{Research questions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (decision shift).} Do open-weight LLMs change accept/reject decisions or \pyes{} when only a caste-coded surname, religion-coded name, region-coded name and hometown, or gender-coded first name is changed?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (language).} Does the shift differ when the same application is written in Hindi instead of English?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (disclosure and self-prediction).} When a decision changes after an identity swap, (a)~does the model's explanation name the attribute, and (b)~asked directly, does the model correctly predict whether its decision would change?
\item \textbf{RQ4 (scale).} Do these effects change across sizes of one model family?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Headline (RQ3):} to our knowledge, the first measurement of whether LLM decision explanations disclose, and whether models can predict, decision changes caused by \emph{implicit, name-coded Indian identity signals}, in English and Hindi. We report disclosure on identity-driven flips against a baseline of flips caused by same-group name swaps, a correlational counterfactual test~\cite{siegel2024probabilities} that uses all pairs, and self-prediction scored against the model's own paired behaviour with a non-introspective baseline.
\item \emph{Precondition (RQ1, RQ2):} a paired audit of eight open-weight models on yes/no allocation decisions with a continuous \pyes{} readout, matched same-group controls as a noise floor, a two-way bootstrap over profiles and names, and a manipulation check that separates ``the model is consistent'' from ``the model did not decode the name''.
\item \emph{Setting (RQ4):} a within-family comparison across Qwen3 4B, 8B, 14B and 32B.
\item A benchmark of 6{,}600 paired prompts per model (released under gated access) and an inference-only toolkit with on-disk caching.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Positioning.} Our RQ3 design follows Arcuschin et al.~\cite{arcuschin2026biases}, who already combine paired decision bias with verbalisation on hiring, loan and admissions tasks; our difference is the implicit Indian signals, Hindi, and the self-prediction probe. Rao et al.~\cite{rao2025invisible} and Zaveri and Shah~\cite{zaveri2025caste} audit Indian-name or caste-surname effects in hiring with a single closed model and do not analyse explanations. RupeeBias~\cite{nair2026rupeebias} covers Indian axes in English and Hinglish with explicit self-labels and numeric outputs rather than names and decisions. Christian and Mazor~\cite{christian2026self} ask models to simulate their own counterfactual behaviour on Tamkin-style decisions with explicit race and gender; our probe asks a direct per-pair question about a name change and scores it against the measured flip.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{LLM decision audits and correspondence studies}
Name-based correspondence audits~\cite{bertrand2004emily} have been carried to India for caste and religion~\cite{banerjee2009labor,siddique2011evidence} and to credit~\cite{kumar2019caste}. LLM audits use templated decision prompts~\cite{tamkin2023evaluating}, resumes and hiring~\cite{an2024large,armstrong2024silicon,wilson2024gender,gao2026llms,seshadri2025small,tan2026small}, mortgage underwriting~\cite{bowen2025measuring}, and name-based advice~\cite{salinas2024whats}. Counterfactual fairness~\cite{kusner2017counterfactual,garg2019counterfactual} and perturbation analysis~\cite{prabhakaran2019perturbation,huang2020reducing,chaudhary2025certifying} provide the paired framing. Seshadri et al.~\cite{seshadri2025small} find models nearly as sensitive to non-demographic edits as to demographic ones, which motivates our same-group controls; prompt-format sensitivity~\cite{sclar2024quantifying} motivates our two templates.

\subsection{India-centric and multilingual bias}
Benchmarks measure caste, religion and regional stereotypes in English~\cite{khandelwal2024indian,sahoo2024indibias,nawale2025fairi,vijayaraghavan2025decaste,seth2025deep} and in Indian languages~\cite{tomar2025bharatbbq,hada2024akal,vashishtha2023evaluating,singh2026inspect}; see~\cite{gamboa2025social} for a multilingual survey. Closer to allocation, studies audit caste in hiring conversations~\cite{dammu2024uncultured}, matchmaking~\cite{naik2026sima}, hiring scenarios with disability~\cite{phutane2026ableist}, financial guidance~\cite{nair2026rupeebias,mahajan2026ifaab}, tutoring explanations~\cite{gupta2026compounding}, legal models~\cite{girhepuje2023legal}, and name-based hiring~\cite{rao2025invisible,zaveri2025caste}. None measures whether explanations disclose decision changes.

\subsection{Explanation faithfulness and self-knowledge}
Explanations can omit the true cause of an answer~\cite{turpin2023language,lanham2023measuring,arcuschin2025chain,chen2025reasoning,karvonen2025robustly}. Faithfulness can be tested with counterfactual edits~\cite{atanasova2023faithfulness,matton2025walk}, correlational tests that use probability shifts~\cite{siegel2024probabilities,siegel2025verbosity}, or self-consistency checks~\cite{parcalabescu2024measuring,madsen2024self}; Parcalabescu and Frank argue such tests measure self-consistency rather than access to mechanisms, which is how we interpret ours. Self-explanations do help predict behaviour on average~\cite{mayne2026positive}, and explanation style itself can differ across groups~\cite{veldanda2026explanation}. Work on introspection and counterfactual simulatability~\cite{binder2025looking,chen2024models,christian2026self} motivates our self-prediction probe.

\subsection{LLM-as-judge}
LLM judges are widely used~\cite{zheng2023judging,gu2024survey} but are position- and prompt-sensitive~\cite{wang2024large} and should be validated with chance-corrected agreement rather than raw accuracy~\cite{thakur2025judging,bavaresco2025llms}. First-token probabilities can disagree with generated text~\cite{wang2024answer}, which we check for both the audited models and the judge.

\section{Benchmark Construction}

\textbf{Scenarios.} S1: shortlisting a final-year B.Tech graduate for an entry-level software-engineer interview. S2: approving a small unsecured personal loan at an Indian bank. S3: awarding a merit-cum-means scholarship (family income below INR~8~lakh is eligible) to a student entering undergraduate study. Each application is a structured form with a name, a hometown, an age and five scenario attributes: college band, CGPA, coding-test percentile, projects and internships (S1); income, existing EMI burden, CIBIL credit score, job tenure and requested amount (S2); Class~10 and~12 marks, aptitude-test percentile, family income and extracurriculars (S3).

\textbf{Profiles.} For each scenario a seeded generator draws 50 profiles: 13 \emph{strong} and 12 \emph{weak} (all five attributes in the strong or weak band, with one attribute set to mid in about half), and 25 \emph{borderline} (13 mixing strong, mid and weak attributes; 12 with all five at mid). Borderline profiles are over-sampled because differential treatment concentrates at the decision margin~\cite{bowen2025measuring}. This gives 150 profiles.

\textbf{Identity variants.} Each profile is rendered with a reference identity and ten variants (Table~\ref{tab:variants}). The reference is a male name with a North-Indian, Brahmin-coded surname and a hometown in Uttar Pradesh. Each identity variant changes one signal: the first name only (gender), the surname only (caste), the full name (religion), or the full name and hometown (region). Two same-group controls change the surname only (\texttt{control\_surname}) or the first name and surname (\texttt{control\_fullname}) to other Brahmin-coded names, keeping the hometown. Each cell has 8 names, aligned by position so that, for example, position~$k$ of the caste cells keeps the first name of reference position~$k$; each profile is assigned one name position, cyclically and stratified by scenario and tier. Christian names are Biblical given names and surnames, which may also signal foreignness; the manipulation check measures this.

\textbf{Name sources.} The name lists were drafted from Wikipedia surname and community pages, the central OBC list for Uttar Pradesh, and the target thresholds of three name-classification tools: Outkast (SC share by surname from SECC-2011), Naampy (gender of first names) and Pranaam (Muslim association). \textbf{Templates and languages.} Each scenario has two paraphrased instruction templates (T1, T2) that end with ``Answer with only one word: Yes or No'' or an equivalent. Every prompt exists in English and Hindi. The Hindi templates were machine-translated by the team lead and were not validated by a native speaker at run time. In Hindi prompts the names and hometowns stay in Latin script, and the answer instruction keeps the English answer words (``Yes \hindi{या} No'') (Appendix~\ref{app:templates}). In total: 150 profiles $\times$ 11 cells $\times$ 2 templates $\times$ 2 languages $=$ 6{,}600 decision prompts per model.

\textbf{Pass 1: decision and \pyes{}.} We read the top-20 log-probabilities of the first generated token and sum the probability of tokens that normalise to \emph{yes}/\hindi{हाँ}/\hindi{हां} and \emph{no}/\hindi{नहीं}; $\pyes = p_\text{yes}/(p_\text{yes}+p_\text{no})$~\cite{tamkin2023evaluating}. In Hindi prompts some tokenisers split \hindi{हाँ} into byte pieces, so the first token misses part of the mass. Wherever the first-token Yes+No mass was below 0.9, we instead score each full candidate string (Yes, No, \hindi{हाँ}, \hindi{हां}, \hindi{नहीं}, each with and without a leading space, deduplicated by token sequence) by its sequence log-probability after the prompt and normalise the summed Yes and No probabilities. \TODO{share of prompts per model and language rescored this way}. The decision is $D=\mathbf{1}[\pyes\ge 0.5]$. As a validity check~\cite{wang2024answer}, we report agreement between $D$ and the first word of the greedy text \TODO{agreement range, English}.

\textbf{Pass 2: reason-first explanations.} The same prompt is re-issued with the answer instruction replaced by ``First give your reasoning in at most 80 words, starting with `Reason:'. Then, on the final line, write `Decision: Yes' or `Decision: No'.'' Placing the reason first avoids a purely post-hoc rationalisation of a fixed answer. We generate explanations for every flipped pair and a seeded 25\% sample of unflipped pairs (both members of each pair), with flips determined from the first-token readout available at run time \TODO{number of pairs whose flip status changed after full-candidate rescoring and that therefore lack explanations}. Generation was capped at 256 tokens; explanations that hit the cap (mostly Hindi) were regenerated with a 768-token cap and the longer version is used. We parse the explanation's final decision and report results both for all flips and for \emph{replicated} flips, where the explanation-pass decision of the variant agrees with its decision-pass decision.

\textbf{Mention detection.} The judge (Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501) labels each explanation blind to the variant: (A)~mentions gender, religion, caste or community, or region or state of origin, explicitly or by clear implication; (B)~mentions only the name, hometown or personal background; (C)~neither (prompt in Appendix~\ref{app:judge}). The label is the argmax of first-token probability over A/B/C. \emph{Strict} mention is A; \emph{lenient} mention is A or B. A keyword detector (attribute terms in English and Hindi) is reported as a check, together with explanation length, since keyword detection is length-sensitive~\cite{siegel2025verbosity}. Judge validity against 200 human labels is pending \TODO{Cohen's $\kappa$ overall, per language, per attribute}.

\textbf{Pass 3: self-prediction.} A two-turn follow-up: turn~1 is the reference prompt, the assistant turn is the model's own reference decision (Yes/No), and turn~2 asks whether the decision would change. In condition \textbf{N} (name) the question gives only the counterfactual name and hometown: ``Now suppose the applicant's name were \emph{X} and the hometown were \emph{Y}, and everything else stayed exactly the same. Would your decision be different?'' Condition \textbf{A} (attribute) additionally states the attribute (e.g.\ ``from a Scheduled Caste (SC) community, for example named \emph{X}\ldots''). Both conditions are asked for every identity and control variant in both languages (12{,}000 prompts per model). The predicted probability of change is read from first-token Yes/No mass, falling back to the parsed text when that mass is below 0.5.

\textbf{Manipulation check.} For each of the 88 names (11 cells $\times$ 8) we ask, in both languages and with forward and reversed option order plus ``Cannot tell'', for the most likely religion, caste category, region of origin, gender, nationality (Indian/not Indian) and socioeconomic background of a person with that name and hometown (2{,}112 prompts per model). We report the share decoded to the intended group on the swapped dimension, the not-Indian share, and the lower-income share. Model-recognised group membership is a measure of signal strength, not a claim about any person.

\textbf{Statistics.} For a pair (profile $p$, template, language, variant $v$), $\dP = \pyes_v - \pyes_\text{ref}$ and a \emph{flip} is $D_v\neq D_\text{ref}$. Per attribute we average $\dP$ over templates and languages to one value per profile. Confidence intervals are 95\% percentiles of a two-way bootstrap ($B=10{,}000$) that resamples profiles within scenario and, independently, the 8 name positions, and multiplies the weights, so that both applicant and name sampling variance are reflected. The primary RQ1 test is a profile-level sign-flip permutation test of mean $\dP=0$ (10{,}000 permutations), Holm-corrected over the 8 identity attributes within each model. Each attribute is also compared with its matched control (\texttt{control\_surname} for gender and caste, \texttt{control\_fullname} for religion and region), via the excess $\dP_v-\dP_\text{ctrl}$, the excess $|\dP|$, and flip rates. For RQ2 we estimate the Hindi-minus-English difference in excess $\dP$ and declare equivalence when its 95\% bootstrap CI lies within $\pm2$ percentage points (more conservative than the 90\% CI of a standard TOST). For RQ3a the correlational counterfactual test (CCT)~\cite{siegel2024probabilities} is the correlation between $|\dP|$ and strict mention over all explained identity pairs. For RQ3b we report balanced accuracy of the predicted change against actual flips, and AUROC of the predicted change probability, compared with the AUROC of a non-introspective baseline $-|\pyes_\text{ref}-0.5|$ (pairs near the margin flip more often). RQ4 is descriptive across the four Qwen3 sizes.

\section{Ethics and Limitations}
\label{sec:ethics}

\textbf{Names as signals.} Names, surnames and hometowns are used only as signals that a model may associate with a group. We make no claim that any person with a given name belongs to, or behaves like, a group. Results are reported only as aggregates per cell; we do not publish per-name results. Because the benchmark encodes caste and religion associations, it will be released under gated access with a usage statement restricting it to auditing and research.

\textbf{Normative framing.} We describe measured effects as \emph{differential treatment}, not as discrimination, since a paired shift establishes that a signal changed the output but not that the change is unjust. This matters most for the scholarship scenario: Indian public policy reserves seats and scholarships for SC, ST and OBC students, so a shift towards SC- or OBC-coded applicants in S3 may reflect a model's learned reservation norm rather than bias in the pejorative sense, although our prompt describes a private merit-cum-means scholarship with an income criterion and no reservation. We therefore report S3 caste results separately and do not label any direction as harmful by default.

\textbf{Validity of the signals.} Name lists were drafted from public sources but had not been checked with the planned classification tools or reviewed by the team when the GPU runs started; the team waived this checkpoint, and the same applies to the Hindi translations. Results may change after validation; because outputs are cached per prompt, corrected names or translations require re-running only the affected prompts. Names also carry class and regional signals beyond the intended attribute~\cite{gaddis2017black}; the South and Northeast pools are Brahmin- or caste-Hindu-coded, and the Christian pool uses Biblical names that may read as non-Indian. The socioeconomic and nationality items of the manipulation check measure some of these confounds but do not remove them. Each cell has only 8 names, so name-level idiosyncrasies can drive cell effects; the two-way bootstrap widens intervals accordingly but cannot fix a small name sample. The gender swap is compared with a surname-swap control, which is an imperfect noise floor for first-name changes.

\textbf{Language.} Hindi prompts are machine translations with names and hometowns in Latin script, so RQ2 does not test Devanagari names, and translation quality may differ across scenarios.

\textbf{Design scope.} Profiles are synthetic, structured forms rather than real applications; the three scenarios and two templates do not cover the space of prompts~\cite{sclar2024quantifying}; we use a single greedy decoding setting with no system prompt, and models deployed with other prompts or sampling may behave differently. Flip status is a threshold on \pyes{} and can be sensitive to small changes near 0.5; we report continuous $\dP$ alongside flips. Explanations were sampled using the first-token readout before rescoring. Mention detection relies on an LLM judge whose agreement with humans is not yet established; we will report Cohen's $\kappa$ on 200 human-labelled explanations. Self-prediction is a verbal report after a fixed assistant turn and measures self-consistency, not access to internal mechanisms~\cite{parcalabescu2024measuring}. We audit only open-weight models up to 32B.

\textbf{Human subjects.} No human-subjects data were collected. The only human input is annotation by the four authors.
