Abstract

The paper audits eight open-weight large language models (Qwen3 4B–32B, Granite-3.3-8B, OLMo-3-7B, Ministral-8B, Sarvam-M) to determine whether the models’ yes/no decisions on applications are shifted by the name-coded Indian identity of applicants, and whether the models’ explanations or self-predictions reveal the shift. On 150 synthetic job, loan, and scholarship applications in English and Hindi, the models’ decisions on paired variants with only a gender-, religion-, caste-, or region-coded signal (a name; for region, name and hometown) were compared with the models’ decisions on same-group name swaps. While the models’ shifts in P(Yes) were small (every model–attribute mean |ΔP| ≤ 2.16 pp), female- and Northeast-coded names shifted the models’ decision flips towards Yes in 7 of 8 models. Moreover, no explanation of an identity-flipped decision named the attribute (0 of 576 explained flips, judged by an LLM), and the models’ predictions of whether a name change would alter their decision were not distinguishable from chance near the decision threshold; over all pairs, two models exceeded chance, but the decision margin predicted flips far better. A positive control that stated identity explicitly showed that the measurement can detect both effects: explicit caste labels shifted P(Yes) by up to 9.72 pp, flipped decisions only towards Yes, and were named in 15% (OBC) and 26% (SC) of explanations on average. On the other hand, caste-coded surnames produced no Holm-significant shift on the primary test (one model differed from its own control on a secondary test), and in English every model answered “Cannot tell” when asked for a name’s caste; the name-coded caste null was therefore best read as non-perception in English, not as evidence of caste-neutral decisions. Finally, explanation review and self-report cannot replace paired behavioral audits.

Introduction

Many large language models (LLMs) are now being used to make or support allocation decisions, such as who is shortlisted for a job, who gets a loan, and who receives a scholarship. In addition, these models are routinely asked to explain their decisions. Anyone relying on such a system must ask two questions. First, does the decision depend on who the applicant appears to be? Second, if the decision does depend on the applicant’s appearance, will the model’s explanation, or the model itself when asked, reveal it? If the first question is answered yes and the second question is answered no, then reading the explanations gives false reassurance.

Correspondence studies in India, such as those by Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) on employment applications, Banerjee et al. (2009) on credit applications and Siddique (2011) on labor markets, have studied differential treatment based on caste and religion coded in names of applicants. This approach has also been used for LLMs, most recently by Tamkin et al. (2023), An et al. (2024), Armstrong and Marks (2024) and Salinas et al. (2024), mostly in the context of US race and gender signals. Indian identity signals are different from US race and gender signals: caste and religion are mostly inferred from surnames, while region is inferred from names and hometowns. Fairness work on India has mostly considered caste, religion, region and gender as key axes of disparity (Sambasivan et al., 2021; Bhatt et al., 2022). Prior LLM audits on Indian names have found that there are no significant caste or region effects in the hiring evaluations of Indian-name swaps using GPT-4o (Rao et al., 2025), while another study using ChatGPT found that dominant-caste names are shortlisted more frequently than other names (Zaveri et al., 2025).

Many works have studied the explanation faithfulness of chain-of-thought and post-hoc explanations in various settings. These works show that explanations can fail to mention the reason(s) why a model answered as it did (Turpin et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025; Matton et al., 2025). Arcuschin et al. (2026) studied whether models verbalize concepts that significantly affect hiring, loan and admissions decisions. Karvonen and Marks (2025) studied the robustly measured race and gender biases in realistic hiring settings and found that the models’ chain-of-thought explanations did not show any signs of such biases. We are studying implicit Indian signals (a name and a hometown) which are first decoded by the model and then can be acted upon or reported by the model. We add a direct self-prediction probe in addition to the explicit-label positive control in order to test whether the disclosure measure can fire at all.

Research questions. RQ1: Do the decisions of the models or the P(Yes) of the models change when the gender-, caste-, religion- or region-coded name of an applicant is changed? RQ2: Is there a difference in effect between English and Hindi prompts? RQ3: When a name change leads to a change in decision, (a) does the explanation mention the name of the attribute, and (b) can the model predict whether the name change would lead to a change in decision? RQ4: Does the effect change with the size of the model family?

The first contribution of this work is the paired audit of eight open-weight models on yes/no allocation decisions with Indian name-coded identity signals in English and Hindi. The audit is done with a continuous P(Yes) readout, matched same-group controls, a two-way bootstrap over applicants and names, and a manipulation check of whether the models are decoding the signals correctly. The second contribution is the joint measurement of whether the models’ explanations disclose the decision changes caused by the implicit Indian identity signals, and whether the models can predict the decision changes. The third contribution is the explicit-label positive control that shows that the same models and judge can act on the stated identity, and sometimes even name the stated identity, so the null under names is informative.

Related Work

LLM decision audits. Correspondence audits have been used in India to study the caste-based differences in labor market outcomes (Banerjee et al., 2009; Siddique, 2011). Recently, several studies have audited LLM decisions using templates of yes/no decision questions (Tamkin et al., 2023; An et al., 2024; Armstrong and Marks, 2024; Salinas et al., 2024), resumes and hiring (An et al., 2024; Armstrong and Marks, 2024; Wilson et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2026; Seshadri et al., 2025), mortgage underwriting (Bowen et al., 2025) and name-based advice (Salinas et al., 2024). Many of these studies have used the framework of counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al., 2017; Garg et al., 2019; Prabhakaran et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2020) to study how decisions change under counterfactual edits to non-demographic attributes of the applicants.

India-centric and multilingual bias. Benchmarks for caste, religion and regional stereotypes are available in English (Khandelwal et al., 2024; Nawale et al., 2025; Vijayaraghavan et al., 2025), English and Hindi (Sahoo et al., 2024) and Indian languages (Tomar et al., 2025). Biases based on gender have been studied in Hindi and multilingual settings (Hada et al., 2024; Vashishtha et al., 2023). A survey of multilingual bias work is provided by Gamboa et al. (2025). Other relevant work on allocation includes work on caste in generated hiring conversations (Dammu et al., 2024), work on financial guidance with explicit self-labels (Nair et al., 2026), and work on Indian-name or caste-surname effects in hiring, with closed models (Rao et al., 2025; Zaveri et al., 2025).

Explanation faithfulness and self-knowledge. Recent studies have found that explanations can fail to include the reason(s) why a model answered as it did (Turpin et al., 2023; arcuschin2025chain; chen2025reasoning; Matton et al., 2025). Furthermore, answers on some tasks depend little on the stated reasoning (Lanham et al., 2023). Counterfactual edits can be used to test faithfulness (Atanasova et al., 2023; Matton et al., 2025), as can correlational tests that use probability shifts (Siegel et al., 2024) or self-consistency checks (Madsen et al., 2024). Parcalabescu and Frank (2024) argue that such tests measure self-consistency, and we interpret ours that way. Self-explanations help predict behavior on average (Mayne et al., 2026), and explanation style can differ across groups (Veldanda et al., 2026). Fine-tuned models can predict properties of their own behavior (Binder et al., 2025). Christian and Mazor (2026) asked models to simulate the decision they would have made had the explicitly stated race and gender been unknown; our probe asks a direct per-pair question about a name change and scores it against the measured flip.

LLM-as-judge. LLM judges are widely used [CITE: zheng2023judging, gu2024survey] but are position- and prompt-sensitive [CITE: wang2024large]. They should be validated against human labels [CITE: bavaresco2025llms] preferably with chance-corrected agreement [CITE: thakur2025judging]. First-token probabilities can disagree with generated text [CITE: wang2024answer], which we check.

Methodology

Benchmark creation

Scenarios and profiles. S1: Shortlist final-year B.Tech graduate for software-engineer interview. S2: Approve small unsecured personal loan. S3: Award private merit-cum-means scholarship (family income < 8 lakh INR is eligible). All applications are structured forms containing name, hometown, age and 5 scenario attributes (e.g. income, EMI burden, CIBIL score, job tenure, requested amount for S2). For each scenario, we draw 50 profiles from a seeded generator: 13 strong, 12 weak (all 5 attributes in that range, and roughly half of them with 1 attribute set to mid), 25 borderline (13 mixed, 12 all mid). We oversample the borderline profiles, since they can flip. This gives us 150 profiles.

Identity variants. Each profile is generated with reference identity and 10 variants (Table [REF: tab:variants]). Reference: male name, North-Indian, Brahmin-coded surname, hometown in UP. For each identity variant, we vary one signal: first name (gender), surname (caste), full name (religion) or full name and hometown (region). We also have 2 same-group controls where we vary either the surname, or the first name and surname to other Brahmin-coded UP names. There are 8 names per cell, indexed by position. For female/OBC/SC/surname-control cells, position k preserves the first name (female: surname) of reference position k. For UP cells, reference hometown is shared at position k. We assign 1 position to each profile cyclically, stratified by scenario and tier. So all effects are relative to 1 privileged intersection (Brahmin-coded, male, UP). We intend for South names to be Brahmin-coded and Northeast names to be caste-Hindu-coded (Assamese). So "region" roughly keeps caste fixed. (But the automated checks don't cover these cells.)

Name lists. We compiled lists from Wikipedia and central OBC list for UP. We targeted thresholds for name-classification tools (Appendix [REF: app:names]). At run time, the name lists and Hindi translations had not yet been validated by the authors. Validation is ongoing. A post-hoc automated check (Outkast: SECC-2011 surname shares, Naampy: electoral-roll gender shares) flags 15/88 names as below target or ambiguous (for 33 more, at least 1 check could not be run). We report results without them as a robustness check.

Templates and languages. For each scenario, we have 2 paraphrased instruction templates (T1, T2). These end with 1 word Yes/No instruction. All prompts are available in English and Hindi. The Hindi prompts (wrappers, forms, instructions) were machine translated. Names, hometowns are kept in Latin script. Answer words are kept as "Yes या No" (Appendix [REF: app:templates]). In total, we have 150 profiles x 11 cells x 2 templates x 2 languages = 6,600 decision prompts per model.

Models and inference

We audit 8 instruction-tuned open-weight models (downloaded only from the creating organisation's official Hugging Face repository and pinned to a commit) (Table [REF: tab:models]). RQ4 family is Qwen3 at 4 sizes. Sarvam-M is Indic-focused. We disable thinking for Qwen3, Sarvam-M. We planned to use 2 Mistral models, but they didn't load in our vLLM image. So we use Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410 and (as judge) Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501. For all passes, we use vLLM (ROCm build, v0.29.1.dev0) on 1 AMD MI300X. Greedy decoding (temperature 0, seed 20261030). We use each model's chat template, without supplying system message (default is kept for each template). 4,096 tokens context. At most 4, 4, 2 tokens for decisions, self-predictions, manipulation checks. At most 256 (384 for positive control) for explanations. We had 131.6 minutes of total logged GPU time across 42 logged jobs (including 9 failed launches).

Passes

Decision pass, P(Yes). We read top-20 log probabilities of the first generated token. We sum the probability of tokens that normalise to yes/हाँ/हां, no/नहीं/नही. P(Yes) = p_(yes)/(p_(yes) + p_(no)) [CITE: tamkin2023evaluating]. Some tokenisers break हाँ into byte pieces. So the first token may not capture all the mass. For any first-token Yes+No mass < 0.9, we instead score each full candidate (Yes, No, हाँ, हां, नहीं, with/without leading space) using its sequence log probability. We normalise the sum of Yes and No probabilities. We do this for all 3,300 Hindi items for Granite. For Sarvam-M, 2,398/3,300. For Ministral, 23. (No English item < 0.9) (Table [REF: tab:models]). Decision is D = 1[P(Yes) >= 0.5]. As a validity check [CITE: wang2024answer], D agrees with whether the 4-token greedy English text starts with "Yes" for 99.0-100.0% of items for each model.

Explanation pass. We re-issue the prompt, replacing answer instruction with a reason-first instruction: Give reasoning in at most 80 words, starting with "Reason:". Then "Decision: Yes" or "Decision: No" on last line. We generate explanations for both members of each flipped pair. And for a seeded 25% random sample of unflipped pairs (identity and control variants alike). Pairs were selected using the first-token readout (available at run time). For the final readout, 576/665 identity flips have explanations (Granite: 38/82, Sarvam-M: 36/74, Ministral: 37/44. All flips for other models.) For explanations that hit the 256-token cap (mostly Hindi), we regenerate with 768-token cap. 302 Granite and 2 Qwen3-8B Hindi explanations are still truncated. We report all explained flips. And replicated flips (explanation's own final decision agrees with decision pass. We parse the first "Decision: Yes/No" in English only. If there isn't one (e.g. some Hindi ones), we count as No.)

Mention detection. Judge (Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501) labels each explanation without seeing the variant. (A) Explicitly or by clear implication, mentions gender, religion, caste/community, or region/state of origin. (B) Only mentions name, hometown or personal background. (C) Neither. (Appendix [REF: app:judge]). We take the first-token argmax over A/B/C as the label. Disclosure is label A. Label B fires if the explanation mentions the applicant's name. So we don't report (A or B) rate. As a judge-independent check, we use an attribute-specific keyword detector to search each explanation for words related to the swapped attribute (English and Hindi. English only for positive control. E.g. female, caste, जाति, state.) Human validation of the judge is not yet done. (agreement with 200 human labels pending. Cohen's κ: [TODO: kappa].)

Self-prediction pass. 2-turn follow-up. Turn 1: reference prompt. Assistant turn: model's reference answer (Yes/No) from first-token readout (at run time. For some Hindi Granite, Sarvam-M, Ministral prompts, this is different from final readout. But it's always English word.) Turn 2: Do you change decision? In condition N (name), we only provide the counterfactual name and hometown in turn 2. "Now suppose the applicant's name were X and hometown were Y. Everything else in the application is exactly the same. Would you make a different decision?" In condition A (attribute), we also state the attribute (e.g. "from Scheduled Caste (SC) community. E.g. named X..."). We ask this for all identity, control variants in both languages (12,000 prompts/model). Predicted change score is first-token Yes/(Yes+No) share. For the main analysis, for pairs where total first-token Yes+No mass is < 0.5, we use the parsed text answer (0 or 1). For bootstrap-CI analysis, we drop them. (e.g. 4,772/4,800 Granite pairs kept.)

Manipulation check. For each of the 88 names (11 cells x 8) with hometown, we ask for most likely religion, caste category, region of origin, gender, nationality (Indian/not Indian), socioeconomic background. We use lettered multiple-choice answer. (religion: 4 options. caste: 4. region: 6. gender: 2. nationality: 2. income level: 3. "Cannot tell" always last.) Substantive options in both orders. In both languages. (2,112 prompts/model). We report abstention rate. Model's top option accuracy (this may be "Cannot tell". We call this free-answer accuracy below.) We also report forced choice (ignoring "Cannot tell". We take the most likely other option from top-20 first-token log probabilities. Forced choice is unreliable when abstention is near 100%.)

Positive control: Explicitly state identity

To see if models act on (and judge detects) identity when the model definitely perceives it, we replace name with applicant ID. We replace hometown line with "Self-declared background: Gender: Male; Religion: Hindu; Caste category: General; Home region: North India". We vary 1 field for each of 8 variants (Female; Muslim, Sikh, Christian; OBC, SC; South India, Northeast India). We use English only. We use template T1 only. 150 profiles x 9 variants (including reference) = 1,350 prompts/model. For decisions, we use first-token P(Yes). (We use English, so we don't need full candidate scoring.) For every prompt, we also get reason-first explanation. We judge as above. For intervals, we use 95% percentile CIs from profile bootstrap (5,000 replicates). (We don't resample name positions. No multiplicity correction.)

Metrics and statistics

For a pair (profile, template, language, variant v), ΔP = P(Yes)_(v) - P(Yes)_(ref). Flip is D_(v) != D_(ref). For each attribute, we take the average ΔP over templates, languages. So we get 1 value per profile. For confidence intervals, we use 95% percentiles of 2-way bootstrap (B = 10,000). We resample profiles within scenario. We independently resample 8 name positions. We multiply the weights. For primary test, we use the p-value of 2-way bootstrap for mean ΔP = 0 (two-sided. Plus 1/B). We apply Holm correction across 8 identity attributes within each model (total 64 tests).

Secondary analyses: (i) Flip direction per attribute, pooled over models. Share of flips that are to-Yes. We use profile-clustered bootstrap CI (2,000 replicates). We take the difference from pooled controls. We use pooled binomial test of to-Yes share against 50%. (Holm over 8 attributes. We treat flips as independent.) We use per-model sign test (models with more to-Yes than to-No flips). We resample profiles across scenarios. (ii) Excess over matched control. (control_surname for gender, caste. control_fullname for religion, region.) ΔP_(v) - ΔP_(ctrl). We use Holm per model. We use Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) across all 64 cells. (iii) Sign stability of primary-significant cells. We drop each name position in turn. (iv) ΔP near threshold. (|P(Yes)_(ref) - 0.5| < 0.3). For identity, control swaps. (v) Primary results without 15 flagged names. (We drop pairs where variant or matched reference name is flagged.) For RQ2, we estimate Hindi-minus-English difference in excess ΔP. This is equivalent if 95% CI is within +-2 pp. For language differences, we use same bootstrap. We use Holm per model. For RQ3b, we report predicted-change rate. We report AUROC of predicted-change score against actual flips. (95% CI from 1,000 profile-bootstrap replicates. Condition N. Identity pairs.) We compare this to non-introspective baseline. We score each pair by -|P(Yes)_(ref) - 0.5|. We do this on all pairs. And on |P(Yes)_(ref) - 0.5| < 0.2. (No CI for baseline.)

No explanation mentions the identity signal for RQ3a

[[KEEP TABLE: tab:rq3 - do not edit, will re-insert]]

Label A rate is 0% for all models and all subsets of 12,714 judged explanations of swapped name-coded variants. 576 identity-flipped explanations. Replicated flips. All control-flipped and unflipped explanations. (Table [REF: tab:rq3].) Keyword detector fires on 6 of 576 identity-flipped explanations. All are false positives for identity disclosure. 4 "state" hits in profile text: "state-level Science Olympiad". 1 "stated purpose". 1 restates hometown "Assam". It never fires on gender, religion, caste words. Since label never varies, the correlational counterfactual test [CITE: siegel2024probabilities] is undefined.

Includes attributes with directional evidence. No explanation of 73 female-coded flips, or 112 Northeast-coded flips, mentions gender or region as a reason. Two caveats are important. 1) Identity flip rates are close to control rates. Just reference margin predicts which pairs flip (AUROC 0.88-0.99). Most individual flips are near-threshold events, not cases where identity was "the" cause. Only 317 of 576 flips replicate in the explanation pass. 2) Disclosure is 0% in every name-coded condition. So disclosure on its own is not sufficient to distinguish hidden identity effects from a general style of never discussing identity. See positive control (Sec. [REF: sec:pc]). So the finding is not that each flip was explained unfaithfully. But that explanations never mention the identity signal behind the aggregate shifts. So explanations cannot be used to detect the shifts. (Aggregate effect is established for only some cells.)

Self-predictions are near threshold chance for RQ3b

Each model shows a fixed response style when asked if its decision would change under counterfactual name. (Table [REF: tab:rq3].) Predicted-change rate is 0.2% (Sarvam-M) to 39.5% (Qwen3-4B). Actual flip rate is 0.8-2.7%. Similar to or slightly higher than control flips (no identity change; e.g. Qwen3-8B 14.5% both. Granite 13.4% vs. 8.1%). AUROC for self-prediction on all pairs is far below the baseline of only margin in every model (0.34-0.68 vs. 0.88-0.99). Qwen3-32B, Granite are above 0.5. Qwen3-8B is below 0.5. 8 self-prediction intervals for near-threshold band (where flips concentrate) all include 0.5. Bands have few flips (22-83). Wide intervals. Qwen3-4B point estimate (0.59) is larger than margin baseline (0.46). So we cannot rule out weak self-knowledge for that model. Predicted-change rate increases for most models when we state the attribute (condition A). (E.g. OLMo-3-7B 0.7%→13.1%.) But balanced accuracy does not (0.409-0.528). We find no detectable ability to predict counterfactual sensitivity to names in the near-threshold band. Limited power. Above-chance AUROCs for all pairs (Qwen3-32B, Granite) are far below margin baseline. They mostly reflect the decision margin. This is consistent with probes like this measuring self-consistency, not introspective access [CITE: parcalabescu2024measuring].

Discussion

What we show in the audit. Small effects for name-coded ( ≤ 2.16 pp on average). But directional for female-, Northeast-coded names. No explanation cites the signal. Self-predictions near the threshold are indistinguishable from chance. If an auditor reads explanations or asks the model, there would be no trace of the shift. Only paired behavioural testing reveals the shift. Positive control shows this silence is not an artefact of a measure that never fires. Also shows that explanations miss explicit gender effects. This extends findings that explanations hide bias for explicit or Western signals [CITE: turpin2023language, karvonen2025robustly, arcuschin2026biases] to implicit Indian signals and a direct self-prediction probe.

Perception is neither necessary nor sufficient. Two strongest, most consistent shifts are for decoded signals (gender, Northeast). But Sikh-coded names also tilt to-Yes (51:25). Although decoding is inconsistent. Well-decoded signal does not necessarily shift decisions. (South. 48:51 pooled flips. But Qwen3-32B shifts by -1.60 pp.) Explicit caste labels move decisions up to 9.7 pp. Surnames do not. (Primary test.)

Relation to prior Indian name audits. Like Rao et al. [CITE: rao2025invisible], we find no caste-coded surname effect on our primary test. But this null is with non-perception of caste in English. And one model (Qwen3-32B) shows caste-coded shifts relative to its own control. Differences from studies reporting caste preference [CITE: zaveri2025caste] may depend on how salient the caste signal is.

Ethics and Limitations

Names as signals. We use names, surnames, hometowns only as signals that a model may associate with a group. We make no claim that any person with a given name belongs to or behaves like a group. Results are aggregate per cell. We drafted name lists from public sources without community or expert review. This matters for a sensitive caste taxonomy. We will release the benchmark with gated access for auditing research. Do not interpret findings as showing that LLMs disadvantage any group (including reference group).

Normative framing. We describe effects as differential treatment, not discrimination. Paired shift shows that a signal changed output. Not that the change is unjust. Shift towards SC, OBC-labelled applicants could be learned reservation norm. (Although our scholarship prompt describes a private scheme with income criterion. No reservation.) We did not break down caste results by scenario.

Validity of the signals. We had not validated name lists and Hindi translations at run time. Validation is ongoing. We automatedly flagged 15 of 88 names. Removing them reduces significant cells from 10 to 8. Names contain signals beyond the intended attribute. (E.g. class [CITE: gaddis2017black].) Caste-coded surnames were not decoded in English. Christian-coded names were not read as Christian in English. 4 Sikh first names are gender ambiguous. Region swaps change name and hometown together. Gender swap uses surname swap control. Imperfect noise floor for first name changes. Only 8 names per cell.

Measurement. Granite and Sarvam-M use a different P(Yes) readout for most Hindi items. Confounding RQ2. We selected explanation pairs with the run-time first-token readout. So 89 of 665 final readout identity flips have no explanation. (Granite 44 of 82. Sarvam-M 38 of 74. Ministral 7.) Self-prediction follows an assistant turn from the same run-time readout. Explanation pass decisions often differ from the decision pass. 302 Granite Hindi explanations are still truncated. We have not yet human-validated the judge. So we do not know its agreement with humans yet. ([TODO: kappa]) But positive control shows it fires on explicit identity. Keyword check finds at most 5.3% (Granite) of identity-flipped explanations. Only one judge model was used. Label A requires naming the attribute. An explanation could indirectly encode identity. (E.g. through tone [CITE: veldanda2026explanation].) We did not measure this. Positive control uses only English, T1, first-token P(Yes). Replaces name, hometown with ID, background line. So its effect sizes are not directly comparable to name-coded ones.

Scope. Profiles are synthetic forms. 3 scenarios. 2 templates. Do not cover the space of prompts [CITE: sclar2024quantifying]. We use one greedy decoding setting. Open-weight models up to 32B. Hindi with Latin script names. Limited power for model-level replication of direction with only 8 models.

Human subjects. No human subjects data collected. We drafted name lists with LLM assistance from public sources. Not reviewed by community members. Only planned human input is authors' annotation of judge labels.

Conclusion

We audited eight open-weight LLMs on paired Indian applications. Differing only in a name-coded identity signal. English and Hindi. Only slightly change decisions. But female- and Northeast-coded names tilt flips to-Yes in 7 of 8 models. No explanation of an identity-flipped decision mentions these signals. Self-predictions near the threshold are indistinguishable from chance. Explicit label positive control shows several models (notably Qwen3 family) act strongly on stated caste. Sometimes name it. So silence under names is informative. Caste-coded surname null is best read as non-perception. Not caste-neutral decisions. Explanation review and self-report are not substitutes for paired behavioural audits. Design transfers to other implicit identity signals and languages. Code and gated benchmark: [TODO: release URL].
